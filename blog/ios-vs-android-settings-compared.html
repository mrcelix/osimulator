iOS vs Android settings, compared menu by menu

Both phones can do the same things, but they file those things in completely different places. Here is how the two Settings apps are structured, and what the same feature is called on each.

Two different filing systems

Open Settings on an iPhone and you get one long scrolling list. Connectivity sits at the top, system-wide behaviour in the middle, and near the bottom every installed app that registers a preference gets its own row.

Android takes the opposite approach. Its Settings app is a short list of broad categories such as Network & internet, Connected devices, Apps, Notifications, Battery and Storage, and almost everything lives two or three taps inside one of them.

The practical difference is scanning versus predicting. iOS lets you scroll until you recognise a name; Android asks you to guess which category a feature was filed under, which is fast once you know the map and slow until you do. Where the same task lives on each

Nearly every everyday task exists on both platforms, just shelved differently. Manufacturer skins matter too, so a Samsung Galaxy running One UI will not always match a Pixel running stock Android.

Same feature, different name

Vocabulary is the other trap. Apple labels mobile data Cellular in the United States and Mobile Data in the UK and Australia. It is the same screen; only the regional wording changes.

Android files the equivalent under Network & internet, where the row may read SIMs, Mobile network or Internet depending on the version and the manufacturer.

Sharing is the clearest split. Apple has kept the name AirDrop since 2011, while Google went from Android Beam to Nearby Share and then to Quick Share once Google and Samsung merged their two systems in 2024. Bluetooth is one of the rare areas where both use the same word, as the Bluetooth pairing guide shows.

Fragmentation only cuts one way

Every iPhone on a given iOS version shows the same Settings layout, so a path you learn on one handset transfers to any other. Android vendors rewrite the Settings app, which is why instructions written for a Pixel often fail on a Galaxy, Xiaomi or OnePlus phone.

That difference explains a lot of bad advice online. The Android structure used in our simulator is machine-extracted from the open-source code rather than retyped from memory, and the data provenance page sets out exactly which systems that applies to.

Both platforms put a search field at the top of Settings. On Android it is usually the quickest route to anything buried three levels down.

Which one is easier to learn

For a first phone, iOS wins on predictability. One list, identical across devices, and Apple moves items between releases only rarely.

For anyone who wants control, Android's category structure scales better, because it can absorb far more options without turning into an endless scroll. Notification handling shows this well: Android exposes per-channel switches inside each app, which the notifications guide walks through, while iOS keeps a simpler per-app toggle.

The fastest way to judge for yourself is to tap through both. You can open the real menus of iOS and Android and explore them in the free browser-based simulator without owning either device.

Key points

  • iOS is one long list; Android is a small set of nested categories.
  • Cellular, Mobile Data and Mobile network all describe the same feature.
  • Android menus vary by manufacturer, iOS menus do not.

Frequently asked questions

Why do Android instructions online often not match my phone?

Because each manufacturer redesigns the Settings app. Stock Android on a Pixel, One UI on a Samsung and MIUI or HyperOS on a Xiaomi use different category names and different nesting for the same features.

Is Cellular the same thing as Mobile network?

Effectively yes. Apple uses Cellular in US English and Mobile Data in UK and Australian English, while Android groups the equivalent controls under Network and internet.

Can iPhones and Android phones share files directly?

Not through AirDrop and Quick Share, which do not talk to each other. Bluetooth, a shared cloud drive or a messaging app remain the reliable cross-platform routes.
