Walk the macOS System Settings tree without a Mac

Apple rebuilt the Mac control panel as a single scrolling sidebar, and plenty of long-time users still hunt for switches that moved. This simulator reproduces that sidebar in a browser, so you can open every pane, follow it down six levels, and compare the newer arrangement against the one that shipped before it, without touching a real machine.

How macOS organises Settings

The sidebar is the whole argument. Thirty entries sit in one column, and the order tells you what Apple thinks you touch most: Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Network and VPN at the top, then Battery, Notifications, Sound and Focus, then the catch-all General. Appearance, Accessibility, Control Center and Siri & Spotlight follow, and hardware such as Keyboard, Trackpad, Mouse and Printers & Scanners drops to the bottom. Nothing is grouped under a heading you have to open first. The trade-off is honest: everything is visible, but thirty visible things is a lot of scanning when you only half-remember the name of what you want.

Depth is where it gets interesting. Roughly 1160 rows hide behind those thirty entries, and the tree runs six levels down, so a pane that looks like four switches often opens into sheets and popovers with their own sub-sheets. Privacy & Security and General carry the heaviest load, absorbing settings that used to live in panes of their own. Desktop & Dock, Displays, Wallpaper, Screen Saver and Lock Screen each own a slice of what you would loosely call appearance, which is exactly where newcomers stall: three of those five sound like they could hold the same option, and only clicking settles it.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Network > Firewall

    The Mac firewall lives inside the Network pane rather than beside the security options, which is why people report they cannot find it. Opening it here shows the route once, so you can describe it from memory later instead of hunting through Privacy & Security first.

  • Settings > Notifications > Scheduled Summary

    This is the switch that batches less urgent alerts and delivers them at set times instead of the moment they arrive. It sits above the per-app list, so it is easy to scroll past while looking for a single noisy application.

  • Settings > Battery > Low Power Mode

    Low Power Mode trades background activity and some display refresh for runtime. Finding it matters because the Battery pane also holds health information and an Options sheet, and the three are frequently confused with one another in support conversations.

How this menu got here

The Mac settings app spent two decades as a grid of icons called System Preferences, where you picked a pane and a window opened. The redesign replaced that grid with a scrolling sidebar and a detail column, borrowing the shape of the iPhone and iPad settings app so the three platforms would read the same way. The two versions offered here, 14 Sonoma and 13 Ventura, both use the sidebar, and putting them side by side shows how entries were renamed, split and reordered in the year between them.

Who this is useful for

Help desk staff who talk Mac users through a fix over the phone get the most out of this, because they can read the exact sidebar order aloud while the caller follows along. It also suits Windows users weighing a switch and wanting to see how much of the machine is configurable, trainers building a lesson without a spare laptop, and anyone comparing the Sonoma arrangement to Ventura before an upgrade.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Network > Firewall and expect it to match your laptop.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical laptop, not to a menu tree. The simulator fills those in with obvious sample values. Use them to learn where a figure lives; look at your own laptop to learn what it says.

Questions about macOS settings

Is this the real macOS settings app?

No. It is an independent browser simulator that reproduces the layout and wording of the System Settings sidebar so you can practise finding things. It is not made by, endorsed by or connected to Apple, nothing you toggle affects a real computer, and no setting is saved anywhere.

Why do the network names and device names look made up?

Because they are sample values. Menu names and their order can be reproduced faithfully, but the contents of a device belong to that device: its Wi-Fi list, its paired accessories, its storage figures. Everything of that kind shown here is placeholder text, clearly marked, and should never be read as a real machine's state.

Can I see how Ventura differed from Sonoma?

Yes. Both 13 Ventura and 14 Sonoma are available from the version picker, and you can switch between them while staying in the same place in the tree. That makes it straightforward to spot an entry that was renamed or moved rather than removed, which is the usual cause of confusion after an update.
