How Huawei Watch organises Settings

The order reads like a list of what a watch is for. Bluetooth and Wi-Fi come first, because a watch that has lost its phone is not much of a watch. Watch Faces follows immediately, which no phone system would ever do, and it earns the position: the face is the product for most owners. Then Display and Brightness, Sound and Vibration, and Health. Celia and Apps covers voice and installed software, Notifications handles what the phone forwards, and a plain Settings entry with About beneath it catches the rest. Ten entries, and not one of them is a miscellany.

Health is the entry that separates this from a general-purpose device. Continuous heart rate, Heart rate alerts, SpO2 monitoring, Stress, TruSleep, Activity reminders and Cycle tracking sit together, and each is essentially a decision about how often the watch measures you and how much battery that costs. That single screen is where most of the tuning happens. Elsewhere the depth is shallow by necessity, four levels at the outside, and the friction newcomers hit is the split of duties with the phone app: plenty of configuration for a Huawei watch lives on the handset, not on the wrist.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Health > TruSleep

    Detailed sleep tracking is the feature most often blamed for a watch not lasting the week, because it keeps sensors working overnight. Finding the switch lets you make an informed trade between the depth of the sleep report and how often you reach for the charger.

  • Settings > Display and Brightness > Raise to wake

    Whether the screen lights when you turn your wrist is the difference between a watch that feels responsive and one that drains by lunchtime. It sits beside Screen-on time and AOD style, and the three interact more than people expect.

  • Settings > Watch Faces > Always On Display

    Always On Display is filed under faces rather than under display, which surprises most owners. Worth locating because it is the single largest battery decision on the device and because the entry position explains why searching the brightness screen turns up nothing.

How this menu got here

Huawei's wearables started with a simple list built for a small round screen and grew as sensors were added, with each new measurement needing its own on-or-off control and its own frequency choice. Over time those controls were gathered under one health heading instead of being scattered, and the watch face section moved near the top to match how often people change it. The two versions offered here, HarmonyOS 5 and HarmonyOS 4, let you see that consolidation in the two most recent generations of the interface.

Who this is useful for

Anyone comparing wearables before buying gets a clear look at what is actually adjustable on the wrist versus what needs the phone app. Beyond that: relatives helping an older family member set up sleep and heart rate tracking remotely, staff in a shop who need to answer a health-tracking question without unboxing a unit, and current owners on the older release checking whether the newer one rearranged anything.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Health > TruSleep and expect it to match your watch.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical watch, not to a menu tree. Those values appear here as clearly-marked samples. They are there so the row has the right shape, and they should never be read as a fact about a real watch.

Questions about Huawei Watch settings

Can I change all these settings from the watch itself?

Some of them, yes, but not all. Wearables split configuration between the device and a companion phone app, and this simulator shows the on-watch menu only. If an option you expect is missing from these ten headings, the usual explanation is that the manufacturer put it in the phone app instead.

Is this built from Huawei's source code?

No. When a system is genuinely open source its menu tree can be taken from source and matched one to one, but this layout is reconstructed from the shipping interface. The wording and structure are followed closely, and the simulator is independent, with no affiliation to Huawei and no endorsement from them.

Do the heart rate and battery figures shown mean anything?

No. Any reading, percentage or paired device name you see is a clearly marked sample value used to make the screens look realistic. Measurements belong to a physical watch on a physical wrist, so nothing displayed here describes a real device, a real person or a real battery state.
