Step through the SteamOS settings screens on any browser

A handheld console has one job in its settings: get out of the way. Ten headings, ninety-odd rows and never more than three levels down, all built to be driven with a thumbstick and a couple of face buttons rather than a cursor. Looking at that list in full shows how much a games machine deliberately chooses not to ask you.

How Steam Deck organises Settings

Compare this with a desktop layout and the restraint is obvious. Internet, Bluetooth, Display, Sound, Power, Controller, Storage, System, Security and System Settings cover the whole surface, and each one holds a handful of children rather than a page of them. Display carries Automatic Brightness, Night Mode, Color Profile and Refresh Rate and stops there. Sound offers Output Device and Enable Bass Boost. Power gathers Battery, the sleep timings, Enable Battery Charge Limit and Screen Off in one screen. The design assumes you are three minutes from starting a game and would rather not be reading.

Controller is the entry with real depth of consequence rather than depth of menu. It holds Steam Deck Controller, Layouts and Enable Steam Input, which together decide how every button on the device maps into whatever you are running, including titles that never expected this hardware. That is where the machine stops behaving like a console and starts behaving like a computer. The other seam is between System and System Settings, two neighbouring headings whose names invite confusion, and it is the one place newcomers regularly click twice before finding what they wanted.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Power > Enable Battery Charge Limit

    Capping the charge keeps a battery healthier over years, which matters on a device many people leave plugged into a dock for weeks. It is tucked under Power alongside the sleep timings, so plenty of owners never notice it is offered at all.

  • Settings > Controller > Enable Steam Input

    Steam Input sits between the physical controls and the game, translating one to the other. Turning it off changes how a title sees the hardware entirely, which is why it is the first thing to check when buttons behave strangely in something that was not built for a handheld.

  • Settings > Display > Refresh Rate

    Lowering the refresh rate is one of the most effective ways to stretch a battery, because the whole system can then target a lower frame rate without the result looking uneven. It is a performance control living in a display menu, which is why it gets missed.

How this menu got here

SteamOS grew out of Valve's earlier attempts to put a games platform on desktop Linux, and the handheld version rebuilt the interface around a controller instead of a mouse. Settings were pared back to what can be reached comfortably with a thumbstick, with the full Linux desktop kept behind a separate mode rather than in this list. The two builds offered here, SteamOS 3.6 and 3.5, are consecutive point releases, so expect adjustments and additions inside existing headings rather than a reorganised top level.

Who this is useful for

People deciding between a handheld and a laptop can see exactly how much configuration the console version asks for. Owners troubleshooting battery life get a map of where the relevant switches actually sit, spread across Power and Display rather than gathered in one place. Community moderators writing instructions for a forum can check heading names without reaching for the device, and shop staff can answer questions cold.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Power > Enable Battery Charge Limit and expect it to match your console.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical console, not to a menu tree. Everywhere the simulator shows a value like that it is clearly-marked sample data: a placeholder that tells you where the number appears, not what any real device would say.

Questions about Steam Deck settings

Does this cover desktop mode as well?

No. What you see here is the controller-driven settings menu that appears in game mode, which is where almost all day-to-day changes happen. The full desktop environment sits behind a separate switch on the real hardware and has its own, much larger, set of system tools that this layout does not reproduce.

Can I test settings before buying a Steam Deck?

You can see what the machine asks you to configure, which is a fair proxy for how much fuss it will be. Walk Internet, Display, Power and Controller and you will have covered nearly everything a new owner touches in the first week. Nothing here connects to hardware or changes anything.

What changed between SteamOS 3.6 and 3.5?

Both builds are available and switching between them shows the difference directly. These are consecutive point releases, so changes tend to be options appearing inside existing headings rather than categories moving. That makes the comparison useful mainly for confirming whether a control you read about is present in the build you are running.
