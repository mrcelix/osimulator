Check the headset settings before you put the Quest on

Headset settings are unusual because you configure the room as well as the device. Meta's headset platform gives ten top-level rows, and one of them is about the physical space around you rather than the hardware. Reading that list flat on a monitor, without a headset on your face, is the easiest way to understand what the machine is actually asking you to set up.

How Meta Quest organises Settings

The list opens like a phone, with Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Device, Display and Sound, and then turns into something no phone has. Boundary and Movement Tracking are top-level rows because a headset has to know where the walls are and where your hands are before anything else works. Apps, Accounts and System finish the list. Roughly 194 rows sit under those ten headings, so each one carries more than its short name suggests, and Device in particular collects Power, Controllers, Storage, Passthrough and Auto Wake into a single screen.

Four levels of depth is shallow, which suits an interface you operate by pointing at floating panels rather than scrolling with a thumb. The risk is different from a phone's: not that you get lost, but that you skip a row you needed. Boundary is the clearest example, holding Boundary Sensitivity, Passthrough on Boundary, Set Up Boundary and Space Sense, safety features that only make sense once you have stood in the room. Compared with a tablet's settings, the vocabulary is half familiar and half entirely new.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Boundary > Space Sense

    This lets you see outlines of real objects and people through the virtual view, so a pet or a family member walking into the room does not become a collision. It is the setting most worth turning on before handing a headset to someone who has never worn one.

  • Settings > Device > Passthrough

    Switching the outward cameras on lets you see the room without removing the headset, which is how you find a drink, a keyboard or the door. It sits under Device rather than Display, and that placement catches out people who assume it is a screen setting.

  • Settings > Display > Refresh Rate

    A higher rate makes motion steadier and can reduce the queasy feeling some people get, at the cost of battery. Since comfort in a headset matters more than it does on a phone, this is worth changing early rather than after a bad first session.

How this menu got here

Standalone headsets arrived without an established convention for their settings, so early menus were short and grew as capabilities appeared. Passthrough, room-scale boundaries and hand tracking each added rows, and the platform was eventually given a name of its own to reflect that it had outgrown being an app launcher. The simulator carries v74 and v72, two consecutive software releases; comparing them shows how quickly rows appear on a platform still changing shape. The layout is reconstructed from the shipping interface rather than from source.

Who this is useful for

Someone considering a headset who wants to know what setup involves before spending. Teachers and museum staff who lend headsets to visitors and need a repeatable safety routine. Parents checking what account and app controls exist. It is also useful if you own one already but only ever wear it to play, and have never seen the rows that sit behind the first screen.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Boundary > Space Sense and expect it to match your headset.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical headset, not to a menu tree. Everywhere the simulator shows a value like that it is clearly-marked sample data: a placeholder that tells you where the number appears, not what any real device would say.

Questions about Meta Quest settings

Do I need a headset to use the simulator?

No. It runs in a browser tab and draws a headset-style frame around the menus, so you can read the whole structure on a monitor. Nothing connects to real hardware, and figures like storage or a paired controller name are clearly marked sample values rather than readings taken from a device.

Is this made by Meta?

No, and it is not endorsed by or affiliated with Meta or any other vendor. It is an independent reconstruction of the menu layout, made so people can learn the structure, compare two software versions, and write instructions that match what someone sees when they put the headset on.

Why does the boundary section matter so much?

Because it is the part of the setup that keeps you from walking into furniture. A headset covers your eyes, so the software has to be told where the safe area ends and what to show you when you reach it. Reading those rows before a first session is quicker than learning them mid-game.
