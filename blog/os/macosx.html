Revisit the System Preferences grid of Snow Leopard

Before settings became an endless scrolling list, they were a grid of little icons in a single window, and you learned the picture rather than the words. Twenty-five panes, one hundred and ninety-four rows and never more than three levels means the whole system fits on one screen. Opening that window again in a browser is oddly clarifying.

How Mac OS X organises Settings

The grid arranges panes by rough theme rather than alphabetically, and the themes are visible at a glance. Appearance, Desktop & Screen Saver, Dock, Exposé & Spaces and Language & Text form the personal row. Displays, Network, Sound, Keyboard, Trackpad, Mouse, Print & Fax, Bluetooth and CDs & DVDs make up the hardware block. Accounts, Date & Time, Software Update, Universal Access, Security, Energy Saver, Sharing, Spotlight, Startup Disk, Time Machine and Speech cover the system itself. Each pane is a self-contained window with its own controls, so the design has no shared navigation to learn.

What makes this feel different from a modern settings app is that depth is capped by the window. Three levels is all a pane can hold before it needs a sheet or a separate tab, so options are laid out across the surface instead of stacked below it. Exposé & Spaces packs corner triggers, keyboard shortcuts and virtual desktops into one dense pane. Language & Text mixes region lists with text substitution. Newcomers coming back to it now trip over the split between Security, Accounts and Sharing, three panes that each hold a piece of what a single privacy screen would cover today.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Exposé & Spaces > Enable Spaces

    This is the switch that turns one desktop into several. Virtual desktops were unfamiliar then and are standard now, so seeing the original opt-in framing explains why the feature still behaves the way it does on later machines.

  • Settings > Dock > Genie effect

    Choosing between the two minimise animations was the classic first tweak on a new machine. It does nothing functional, which is precisely why it survives in memory as the thing everyone changed within five minutes of opening the box.

  • Settings > Appearance > Show scroll bars automatically

    Deciding when scroll bars appear looks trivial until a bar vanishes and you cannot tell how long a document is. This row sits in the pane where colour themes live, which is where most people forget to look.

How this menu got here

System Preferences replaced the older stack of separate control files with one window holding a grid of panes, and each release added panes as new hardware and services arrived rather than reorganising what was there. Spaces, Time Machine and Spotlight all show up as their own icons for that reason. The two versions offered here, 10.6 and 10.5, sit either side of a large under-the-hood rewrite that changed relatively little at the surface, so comparing them is mostly an exercise in spotting removed and renamed panes.

Who this is useful for

People maintaining older studio or lab machines still running this generation get practical use, since they can locate a pane before walking to the hardware. Interface students comparing a grid of panes with a modern scrolling list have both open at once. Writers and video makers working on period-accurate scenes can check what a pane actually held, and anyone restoring an old machine can plan the setup order before booting it.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Exposé & Spaces > Enable Spaces and expect it to match your classic Mac.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical classic Mac, not to a menu tree. Those values appear here as clearly-marked samples. They are there so the row has the right shape, and they should never be read as a fact about a real classic Mac.

Questions about Mac OS X settings

Is this the real Snow Leopard System Preferences?

The pane names, their grouping and their contents follow the shipping interface, reconstructed screen by screen rather than taken from published source, because this system is not open source. Anything that would belong to a specific machine, such as display resolutions in use or account names, appears as clearly marked sample values.

What is the difference between 10.6 and 10.5 here?

Mostly panes appearing, disappearing or being renamed, plus small changes inside individual panes. Switch between the two in the picker and the tree reloads so you can look at the same pane in both. The larger differences between those releases were internal and never surfaced in the preferences window.

Why does it look so different from a current Mac?

Preferences were a grid of independent panes rather than one continuous list with a sidebar, and each pane opened as its own screen. The later redesign folded those panes into a single scrolling column borrowed from the phone interface, which is the main reason old click paths no longer match new ones.
