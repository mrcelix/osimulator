Explore the iPhone Settings tree without touching a phone

Apple's phone Settings app is one long scrolling list that mixes hardware switches, per-app preferences and privacy controls in the same column. Thirty-one top-level rows lead to roughly 1,950 individual settings, nested as deep as ten levels. Walking that tree in a browser simulator lets you find where something lives before you go looking on a real handset.

How iOS organises Settings

The ordering tells you what Apple thinks you touch most. Airplane Mode, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, Cellular and Personal Hotspot sit at the very top as a connectivity block. Below them come the attention controls, with Notifications, Sounds & Haptics, Focus and Screen Time grouped as if managing interruptions were a single subject. Then hardware and appearance: Battery, Action Button, StandBy, General, Control Center, Display & Brightness. Only after all of that do individual apps appear, with App Store, Wallet & Apple Pay, Safari, Messages, Phone, Photos and Health each getting a row of its own. Nothing is filed by category name; the order is the category.

Depth is where people lose the thread. Ten levels of nesting means a switch can sit four or five taps below a row you already had to scroll to reach. Cellular alone holds a stack of items including Cellular Data Options, Network Selection, Wi-Fi Calling and SIM PIN, and several of them open further screens. General is the deepest of all, acting as a catch-all for anything that did not earn a top-level row. Compared with Android's grouped Settings, Apple trades tidy categories for a flat first screen, which is faster to skim and harder to memorise.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Cellular > Wi-Fi Calling

    Placing calls over a wireless network instead of the mobile signal is the fix for a house with one bar. It is filed under Cellular rather than Wi-Fi, which is exactly why people fail to find it, and support agents spend minutes describing the route.

  • Settings > Notifications > Scheduled Summary

    Instead of alerts arriving one at a time, this collects the non-urgent ones and delivers them at times you choose. It is the most effective way to quiet a busy phone without silencing it, and it sits above the per-app list where few people scroll.

  • Settings > Sounds & Haptics > Change with Buttons

    This decides whether the side buttons alter the ringer volume or only media volume. Leave it off and your ringtone stays where you set it no matter how many times you nudge the volume down during a video. It explains most complaints about missed calls.

How this menu got here

The list has grown by accretion. Each new capability arrived as another row rather than a reorganisation, which is why Screen Time, Focus, StandBy and Action Button now sit beside the original network switches. Apple periodically folds older rows into General, but the top of the list has stayed recognisable for years. The simulator carries 17.5 and 16.7, two closely related releases that let you see which rows appeared, moved or changed wording between them without owning two handsets. iOS is not open source, so the layout here is reconstructed from the shipping interface.

Who this is useful for

Helpdesk staff who write instructions for callers get the wording right the first time. Teachers and trainers can project the tree on a screen instead of passing a handset around a room. Anyone moving from Android can see how far the two lists diverge before committing. And if you are talking a parent through a change over the phone, you can follow the same taps on your own screen while you speak.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Cellular > Wi-Fi Calling and expect it to match your phone.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical phone, not to a menu tree. Everywhere the simulator shows a value like that it is clearly-marked sample data: a placeholder that tells you where the number appears, not what any real device would say.

Questions about iOS settings

Is this the real iOS Settings app?

It is a reconstruction of the layout, not Apple software and not connected to Apple in any way. Menu titles, their order and their nesting follow the shipping interface. Anything that belongs to a physical handset, such as a network name, a build number or a storage figure, appears as a clearly marked sample value rather than a real reading.

Can I compare iOS 17.5 with 16.7?

Yes. Both versions are available and you can switch between them, so a row that moved or was renamed becomes obvious. There is also a side-by-side comparison mode for putting iOS next to another system entirely, which is useful when you are documenting the same task for two different phones.

Do I need to install anything or sign in?

No. Everything runs in the browser tab with no account, no download and no data leaving your machine. You can also record a click-path through the menus and share it, switch the interface into one of twelve languages, or search for a setting by name when you cannot remember which menu holds it.
