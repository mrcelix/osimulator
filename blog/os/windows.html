Walk the Windows Settings tree without a PC in front of you

Microsoft rebuilt the PC control surface into two columns: a fixed sidebar of eleven categories on the left, and pages of grouped cards on the right. Windows 11 keeps that shape while still handing a few tasks off to older dialogs. Walking the tree in a simulator lets you learn where a setting lives before you touch a working machine.

How Windows organises Settings

The eleven top-level entries read like a map of responsibilities rather than a map of hardware. System, Bluetooth & devices, Network & internet and Personalization sit near the top because they cover what people change most, while Accounts, Time & language, Gaming, Accessibility, Privacy & security and Windows Update trail behind as periodic housekeeping. Apps holds Installed apps, Default apps and Startup together in one place instead of scattering them across the tree. The grouping tells you Microsoft decided an alphabetical control panel behaved like a filing cabinet, and that most people would rather browse by intent than by component name.

Depth is where this system differs from a phone. Five levels is unusual, and the reason is that a category such as System carries ten children of its own, from Display and Sound through Storage, Multitasking and Remote Desktop, each of which can open further pages. Newcomers usually get lost in the overlap: Personalization owns Background, Colors, Themes, Lock screen, Start and Taskbar, yet Device usage also sits there, which feels closer to Accounts. Compared with its own predecessor, the layout is calmer and wider, though a handful of destinations still hand you to a legacy window.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > System > Focus

    Focus silences notifications for a chosen stretch of time and can hide badges while it runs. It sits under System rather than Notifications, which is exactly the kind of placement people fail to guess, so finding it once in the simulator saves a search later.

  • Settings > Apps > Startup

    Startup lists the programs that launch when you sign in and lets you switch them off one by one. It is the first place to look when a machine takes a long time to become usable, and it is far easier to reach here than in the old task manager route.

  • Settings > Accounts > Sign-in options

    Sign-in options gathers the ways you can prove who you are, from a PIN to a security key, along with the rules for when the lock screen returns. Support staff walk people through this page constantly, so rehearsing the path is practical.

How this menu got here

Settings began as a touch-friendly companion to Control Panel and slowly absorbed its jobs, page by page, over several releases. The two versions in the simulator show the two halves of that story. Windows 10 uses a grid of icons that opens into narrow single-column pages. Windows 11 replaces the grid with a permanent left rail, breadcrumbs across the top and expandable cards, so a category such as Bluetooth & devices shows its contents without a second click. Because the interface is not open source, the layout here is reconstructed from the shipping product.

Who this is useful for

Helpdesk staff who describe a path out loud rather than sharing a screen get the most from this. So do teachers preparing a lesson on a class of machines they cannot all touch, and people who left the platform years ago and want to see how far the layout has moved before buying a laptop. Writers producing documentation can also check a route quickly without rebooting into another install.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > System > Focus and expect it to match your PC.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical PC, not to a menu tree. Everywhere the simulator shows a value like that it is clearly-marked sample data: a placeholder that tells you where the number appears, not what any real device would say.

Try it yourself

The useful thing is to go looking for something specific. Pick a setting you would normally hunt for on a real PC, find it here, and record the path while you do it so you never have to find it twice. When the question is really Windows versus something else, open them side by side rather than reading two separate menus from memory. If you already know what the setting is called, the site-wide search will tell you which section hides it here.

Questions about Windows settings

Is this the real Windows Settings app?

No. It is an independent simulator that reproduces the menu layout so you can practise navigation in a browser. It is not made by, affiliated with or endorsed by Microsoft, and nothing you tap changes a real computer. Values such as device names and network names are clearly marked samples rather than readings from your machine.

Which Windows versions can I compare?

The simulator carries Windows 11 and Windows 10, and you can switch between them while staying on the same category. That makes it easy to see where a page moved between releases, which matters when you are writing instructions that have to cover both and cannot assume everyone has upgraded.

Do I need to install or sign in to anything?

No. Everything runs in the browser with no account, no download and no connection once the page has loaded. You can open the Windows layout, tap through System, Personalization or Privacy & security, and close the tab afterwards without leaving anything behind on the computer you used.
