A screen the size of a postage stamp still has to carry twenty-seven top-level categories and around a thousand rows underneath them. Scrolling that list on a desktop display, where you can see several entries at once, makes the structure legible in a way the wrist never quite manages.

How watchOS organises Settings

The top level is long and deliberately flat. Airplane Mode, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and Cellular lead, then Notifications, Sounds & Haptics and Focus, then a long run that includes General, Display & Brightness, Clock, Wallet & Apple Pay, Passcode, Battery and Siri. Health-related entries get their own slots rather than hiding inside a submenu: Workout, Heart and Sleep each sit at the top level, which is a statement about what this device is for. Digital Crown and Sound Recognition appear alongside them, and the effect is a list that reads like a feature inventory rather than a tidy taxonomy.

Ten levels of depth on a watch sounds implausible until you follow a single branch. Notifications alone carries its own switches, such as Notification Privacy and Announce Notifications, and then a per-app run through Messages, Mail, Activity, Heart Rate, Calendar and Phone, each of which opens its own choices. Cellular behaves the same way, moving from the carrier entry to Add eSIM, Data Mode and Data Roaming. Newcomers lose their place because the scroll is long and the back gesture is small, so a flat view on a larger screen usually beats scrolling on the wrist.

Three settings worth finding

  • Settings > Notifications > Notification Privacy

    This decides whether the text of an alert appears before you raise your wrist and look, or stays hidden until then. It is the single switch that determines how much a person standing beside you can read, and it is buried far enough down that many owners never meet it.

  • Settings > Focus > Do Not Disturb

    Do Not Disturb on a watch stops the taps as well as the sounds, which matters more here than on a phone you can leave face down. Setting it from the wrist rather than the phone is worth rehearsing, because the two do not always behave identically.

  • Settings > Sounds & Haptics > Cover to Mute

    Cover to Mute lets you silence an alert by resting your palm over the display for a moment. It is a physical gesture rather than a menu action, and the setting that enables it is easy to miss unless you have gone looking for it deliberately.

How this menu got here

The watch began with settings split awkwardly between the wrist and a companion phone app, and the balance has shifted steadily toward doing more on the device itself. Later releases added standalone entries for health and fitness features, then reorganised the long list so related switches sit together. The two versions offered here, watchOS 10 and watchOS 9, sit either side of a broad visual refresh that changed how apps present information, while keeping the top-level ordering recognisable. Layout is reconstructed from the shipping interface rather than extracted from source.

Who this is useful for

Anyone helping an older relative set up a first watch benefits from seeing the whole list at once instead of scrolling a tiny screen in front of them. Retail staff answering questions about health features can check where Workout, Heart and Sleep actually sit. So can accessibility trainers, and anyone comparing what has to be done on the watch against what still lives on the paired phone.

What is real and what is sample data

The structure is the real thing: menu names, their order, how they are grouped and what sits inside each one. That is why this article can quote a path such as Settings > Notifications > Notification Privacy and expect it to match your watch.

The state cannot be. A device name, an address, a build number, a network name, a storage figure, whether a particular switch is on or off — those belong to a physical watch, not to a menu tree. The simulator fills those in with obvious sample values. Use them to learn where a figure lives; look at your own watch to learn what it says.

Questions about watchOS settings

Can I change my own Apple Watch from here?

No. The simulator is a read-only model of the menu structure running in your browser, with no connection to any device. Entries such as network names, paired accessories and carrier names are sample values chosen to make the screens look realistic, not readings taken from a watch you own.

Why does the watch have so many settings?

Because a watch handles connectivity, health sensors, payments, accessibility and a companion relationship with a phone, all on one screen. The simulator counts roughly a thousand rows across every depth, which is why the top level runs to twenty-seven entries rather than folding everything into a handful of broad categories.

Does it show watchOS 10 and watchOS 9 separately?

Yes. Both versions are available and you can switch between them without losing your place, which is useful when someone describes a screen that has since moved. Comparing the two side by side is often faster than reading release notes, particularly for menus that were renamed rather than removed.
